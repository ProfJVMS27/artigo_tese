Abstract
Designing multifunctional shields for radiological dispersal device (RDD)
response requires understanding how material selection and layer arrangement
influence radiation attenuation, ballistic resistance, and system mass. We
evaluate an uncertainty-aware workflow linking MCNP6.3 photon transport,
LS-DYNA fragment impact, physics-guided features, response-specific surro-
gates, and constrained NSGA-II. A seven-layer design space supplies 2,000
dose calculations and 1,996 accepted penetration labels. Relative to the best
tuned RAW learners, physics-guided representations reduce development-CV
RMSE by 81.9% for dose and 21.4% for penetration. Frozen Ridge and
XGBoost models achieve test𝑅2 values of 0.994 and 0.886, respectively. An
equal-budget benchmark yields mean normalized hypervolumes of 0.7526 for
NSGA-II, 0.7619 for NSGA-III, and 0.7209 for random search with a bounded
archive. Thirty pre-specified designs are regenerated in both solvers. Their
dose and penetration MAEs are 0.0883 mSv and 0.8094 mm, and they collec-
tively dominate 73 of 295 original solver-evaluated Pareto designs. However,
dose is underpredicted in 28 cases and its interval coverage falls to 70%. The
constant conformal penetration margin is nonbinding at the operational 60
mm limit, so a safety benefit from the margin is not demonstrated. The

1. Introduction
1.1. Engineering problem and scope
Designing protection for radiological dispersal device (RDD) response re-
quires distinguishing hazards that share an operational setting but act through
different physical mechanisms. External gamma exposure depends on photon
transport, whereas fragment resistance depends on transient deformation and
failure. Their common design variables are material selection, layer order,
and thickness. A configuration that improves one response can increase mass
or material expenditure without improving the other. Research on polymer
composites, high-density fillers, ceramics, and layered shields motivates this
design space, but attenuation performance alone does not establish the merit
of a multifunctional protective system [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6].
The response setting gives these trade-offs a practical context without
making the present model an operational emergency protocol. RDD guidance
distinguishes external irradiation, radioactive contamination, conventional
explosive hazards, and the associated protective actions [7, 8, 9]. Appropriate
respiratory and dermal protection can reduce contamination, but does not
replaceshieldingagainstpenetratinggammaradiation[ 10,11]. Time, distance,
monitoring, and shielding remain complementary measures. The source-
security record also requires care: incidents involving radioactive material are
not equivalent to confirmed RDD detonations [12].
Within this context, recent consequence studies address a different level
of the response system. Al-Shami et al. linked HotSpot and Geant4 to inves-
tigate probabilistic urban exposure and building shielding [13]. Other work
examines hypothetical urban consequences, monitoring, or digital response
coordination [14, 15, 16]. These approaches help characterize an incident and
its environment. The present work instead concerns pre-deployment design
of a prescribed barrier under a fixed gamma source and a controlled fragment
2
threat. It neither predicts atmospheric dispersal nor substitutes for radiation
surveys or explosive-device assessment.
The distinction is particularly important for the ballistic model. A
fragment-simulating projectile (FSP) provides a controlled impact condition,
allowing comparisons that are difficult to obtain from irregular fragments with
variable mass and orientation [17, 18, 19]. Here, a specified steel cube is used
as the numerical FSP. It is not asserted to reproduce a standardized projectile
shape or every effect of a real grenade. Blast loading, fragment clouds, and the
distribution of impact angles remain outside the model. Likewise, the shield
masses obtained in this study are incompatible with wearable protection. The
relevant interpretation is a substantial barrier requiring support or assisted
handling, not personal body armor or a certified hand-portable device.
1.2. Related methodological work
These physical distinctions motivate separate surrogate models for the
two responses. Recent shielding-specific work includes learning gamma atten-
uation coefficients for scintillator materials [20] and linking dose prediction to
multi-objective reactor-shield optimization [21]. Such studies establish that
learned shielding models are useful without implying that they resolve frag-
ment resistance. General surrogate-modeling methods can reduce repeated
computational evaluations [22], but their use in an optimizer creates an addi-
tional question: whether errors remain acceptable in the designs selected by
the model itself. Ensemble regression and boosting provide useful inductive
biases for tabular engineering data [23, 24, 25, 26]. Their algorithmic maturity
does not remove the need to examine representation, extrapolation, and the
decision consequences of residual error.
Surrogate-assisted evolutionary optimization addresses this broader prob-
lem through model management. ParEGO uses sequential scalarization and
Gaussian-process approximation for expensive multi-objective evaluations
[27]. K-RVEA combines reference-vector search with surrogate uncertainty
and diversity management [28]. These are stronger methodological compara-
tors than an unqualified assertion that any surrogate search is novel. The
present implementation uses offline-trained models and NSGA-II [29, 30]. It
does not introduce a new evolutionary algorithm or claim superiority over
sequential Bayesian optimization. Table 1 positions its components against
representative methodological and application studies.

The comparison identifies a practical role for uncertainty while delimiting
its claims. Split conformal prediction calibrates a residual-based interval
under exchangeability [34, 31]. A constant interval width can be carried into
feasibility as a data-derived additive margin. It is not design-dependent uncer-
tainty. Conformalized quantile regression and weighted conformal inference
address different aspects of heterogeneity and distribution shift [32, 33]. In
4
design problems, the learned predictor can itself determine the evaluation
distribution, creating the feedback dependence studied by Fannjiang et al.
[35]. Those methods require their own assumptions and evaluation protocol
rather than merely a change in terminology.
1.3. Study objectives and contribution
Against this background, the study evaluates three propositions. First,
physics-guided feature engineering should improve prediction relative to
competitive RAW models, not only relative to an unsuitable linear baseline.
Second, the decision value of a calibrated penetration margin must be assessed
from the candidates it actually excludes. Third, optimized designs should be
compared with the original design set using regenerated solver evaluations,
rather than surrogate predictions alone. These objectives are evaluated
through representation benchmarks, threshold diagnostics, and high-fidelity
re-evaluation, respectively.
The contribution is therefore a traceable engineering integration: a
shared seven-layer design space, separate transport and impact labels,
response-specific surrogate calibration, multi-objective search, and numerical
re-evaluation of pre-specified candidates. The study also reports where that
integration falls short. The 60 mm penetration constraint is nonbinding in
the observed search, the dose interval loses coverage on optimized designs,
and solver agreement is assessed within the specified numerical models.
These findings define the useful scope of an uncertainty-aware workflow more
precisely than a claim that uncertainty necessarily improved every design
decision.

2.2. Material library, sampling, mass, and cost
The fixed library contains SiC, UHMWPE, Al, Fe, B4C-HDPE, W-epoxy,
and Bi2O3-HDPE, each used once. The library represents a prescribed multi-
functional architecture, not an unrestricted material-selection optimum. In
particular, a gamma-only calculation cannot demonstrate a neutron-protection
benefit for B4C. Polymeric and ceramic constituents remain in the library
because the architecture is also evaluated mechanically. Their inclusion is a
constraint to be examined in future material-library studies, not a demon-
strated necessity for gamma shielding.

The final radiological dataset contains 2,000 completed DE/DF calcula-

The ballistic dataset contains 1,996 accepted labels and four documented
exclusions. The master retains original and recovered sources rather than
constituting 2,000 uniformly rerun cases. Recovery-stage contact or terminal-
time changes must therefore remain identifiable through provenance. No new
LS-DYNA calculation or label change was made for the present manuscript
revision. The maximum penetration is the largest inward advance of the
recorded FSP front relative to the 410 mm outer surface over the recorded
time horizon. It is not clipped to 60 mm, and an unresolved time history
must not be interpreted as demonstrated arrest.
The excluded identifiers are 733, 832, 1277, and 1567. Their recorded

missingness potentially informative. Their mechanical outputs are excluded,
but their valid MCNP outputs remain usable. Artificially assigning 60 mm
would be a hypothetical stress test, not a recovered observation. The sup-
plement retains the four designs and quality flags to make this limitation

2.5. Optimization, selection, and post-review analyses
The in-project mixed-variable NSGA-II implementation uses a population
of 300 for 200 generations in each of ten runs. Seeds are 42, 137, 233, 349,
457, 563, 677, 809, 929, and 1049. Permutations use order crossover and
swap mutation, while thicknesses use bounded simulated-binary crossover
and polynomial mutation followed by box–simplex projection and 0.001 cm
resolution repair. Crossover probability is 0.9, swap probability 0.20, thickness
mutation probability1/7, and crossover/mutation distribution indices 15 and
20. Feasibility tolerance is10−9 mm. Metrics are recorded every five genera-
tions and checkpoints every 50. These budgets are declared computational
choices, not proven sufficient for global convergence.
Hypervolume uses fixed DoE-derived anchors in dose, mass, and cost units:
𝑧ideal = (4.877620,107.616961,1806.158949),
𝑧ref = (9.360958,251.172670,17812.871711).
The mean terminal-run result is the principal comparison. The union of ten
runs is a secondary accumulated-search result. IGD and generalized spread
use an empirical reference set, not the unknown true front. The 2,000-point
DoE is a starting-design baseline and does not have the same evaluation
budget as NSGA-II. The original campaign normalized and clipped objective
coordinates to[0, 1]. The additional equal-budget benchmark retains the same
anchors but does not clip coordinates better than the DoE ideal. This prevents
improvements beyond the initial design range from being artificially saturated.
Its HV values must therefore be compared within the new benchmark, not
directly with the archived clipped values.
From the 1,530-point union, thirty candidates were selected by minimum
dose, minimum mass, minimum cost, TOPSIS-IQR, and deterministic max-
imin coverage in normalized objective space. The local selection manifest is
timestamped 2026-10-03 10:30:06 UTC and records the source-front SHA256.
This is a pre-specified local selection, not external preregistration. All thirty
regenerated MCNP and LS-DYNA cases are reported, without retaining only
favorable agreements. Their 0.30 ms penetration labels remain the primary
comparison. Two later 0.60 ms cases assess time sensitivity without replacing
those labels.

2.6. Equal-budget optimizer benchmark and margin ablations
To test search strategy separately from surrogate accuracy, an additional
protocol was fixed before its runs and executed after the original candidate
validation. NSGA-II, NSGA-III, and independent random search with a
bounded elitist archive were compared at the operational 60 mm limit with
the frozen margin. NSGA-II was also run without the margin at 60 mm and
with and without it at 15, 20, and 30 mm. The same ten seeds give 100
runs across ten conditions. Each run evaluates 300 initial designs and 200
batches of 300 new designs, totaling 60,300 evaluations. Initial populations
are identical for matching seeds. The tighter limits are decision-threshold
sensitivities, not new physical threats or certified acceptance criteria.
The mixed-variable operators and repair remain unchanged. NSGA-III
uses reference-direction survival from pymoo 0.6.1.5 [40], with 300 Das–Dennis
directions for three objectives and 23 partitions. Its mating tournaments com-
pare constraint violation and break ties randomly. Random search draws new
designs independently using the same permutation and constrained-Dirichlet
sampler. Its archive retains at most 300 designs through nondominated sort-
ing and crowding, without influencing subsequent draws. Thus, all methods
share the evaluation budget and retention capacity. The random archive is
not the complete nondominated set of all sampled designs.
Final-population HV is the primary endpoint. IGD and generalized spread
use a pooled empirical reference front formed separately for each threshold and
margin rule. Their values should not be compared across different reference
sets. Six paired HV contrasts were specified: NSGA-II against each alternative
at 60 mm, and margin against point-only NSGA-II at each threshold. Two-
sided Wilcoxon tests use Holm correction across these six contrasts. Paired
bootstrap intervals use 10,000 resamples of the ten seeds. All models, source
hashes, and run settings are frozen. No additional solver calculations or
surrogate fitting are included in this benchmark.

4. Discussion
4.1. What the engineering integration establishes
The principal benefit of the workflow is not a new learning algorithm. It
is the ability to compare two response models on the same geometry while
preserving their different limitations. The strongest integration result is the
solver-based improvement of the selected objective set: 73 DoE front members
are dominated by at least one re-evaluated design. This result survives
replacement of surrogate dose by MCNP dose. It therefore provides more
direct support for the design workflow than the surrogate-space hypervolume
gain alone.
That improvement is nevertheless conditional on the material library,
numerical scenario, and accounting coefficients. In the union, Spearman
correlations are−0.9953for dose versus mass,−0.6763for dose versus cost,
and+0.6229for mass versus cost. The front is strongly organized by the dose–
mass trade-off rather than equally independent variation in three objectives.
Cost still distinguishes configurations, but the geometry of the objective set
should not be described as evidence of three equally strong conflicts.
Material-position frequencies offer a complementary engineering interpre-
tation. W-epoxy occupies the inner layer in 78.2% of union designs and never
the outer layer. Bi2O3-HDPE occupies layer 2 in 68.1%, while Fe and SiC
occupy the outer layer in 37.9% and 36.1%, respectively. These are patterns
of the frozen-model search, not causal design rules. In particular, their coex-
istence does not demonstrate that the nonbinding ballistic constraint caused
those placements. The complete thirty material sequences and thickness
allocations are provided in the supplement so that individual configurations
can be inspected rather than inferred from an aggregate plot.
Theresultingmasses, 95.15–256.21kg, substantiallynarrowtheapplication
claim. They rule out wearable protection and make manual portability an
unsupported characterization. Supported, wheeled, or vehicle-associated
implementations are possible contexts for later engineering development, but
were not designed or qualified here. A deployable system would require
structural support, manufacturing, handling, ergonomics, and operational
evidence beyond the optimization variables.
24
4.2. Uncertainty awareness without an unsupported safety claim
The fixed conformal allowance has an auditable statistical origin, but its
mathematical role is a constant tightening of the limit. At 60 mm it did
not change eligibility in the inspected DoE and retained fronts. The study
thus demonstrates implementation and calibration of an uncertainty-aware
screen, not a measured safety benefit from that screen. The tighter-threshold
diagnostic shows that the margin can matter in other decision regimes, while
leaving the current engineering requirement untouched.
The dose results reveal why this distinction matters. A model with
excellent random-test accuracy still underpredicts many optimizer-selected
values, and 70% interval coverage is materially below the nominal 95%.
Exchangeability-based calibration does not automatically transfer to adap-
tively selected designs. The boundary enrichment and unseen permutations
identify a plausible distribution-change mechanism, but further data would be
needed to isolate it. Locally adaptive conformal scores or weighted methods
are reasonable future comparisons [32, 33]. Retrospectively enlarging inter-
vals to cover these thirty outcomes and then reporting them as independent
validation would not be a valid solution.
Penetration requires particular caution in its upper tail. The highest test
quartile has substantially worse error and coverage than the aggregate score
suggests, and four failed simulations share thin outer-Al layers. Together,
these findings weaken any attempt to interpret an average𝑅2 or nominal
interval as a near-perforation reliability certificate. The current evidence
supports screening within the numerical domain and direct re-evaluation of
candidates. It does not establish uncertainty in the constitutive laws, contact
treatment, or real fragment spectrum.
4.3. Computational evidence and fair comparison
The archived inference benchmark records approximately 0.409 ms per
case for PHYSICS/Ridge and 0.776 ms per case for PHYSICS-S23/XGBoost,
measured as batch prediction time divided by 300 cases. The ten optimization
runs total 9,814 s, or 2.73 h, on the recorded execution environment. These
timings demonstrate a practical computational workflow. They are not
single-query latencies or a controlled end-to-end speedup against direct solver
optimization. Hardware, batch size, preprocessing, solver tasks, and caching
would need to be matched for such a claim.
The matched-budget benchmark separates improvement over the initial
DoE from improvement attributable to search strategy. NSGA-II exceeds the
25
random-search archive in HV, but NSGA-III improves HV further under the
tested settings. This supports the modularity of the workflow rather than a
claim that NSGA-II is its uniquely preferred optimizer. The result remains
conditional on frozen surrogates and the declared operators, population, and
budget. Importantly, the thirty solver-re-evaluated candidates came from
the original NSGA-II campaign. Their agreement with the solvers cannot
be transferred to the newly generated NSGA-III front. The benchmark did
not retrospectively replace those candidates or select a favorable validation
subset.
For learning, the best-RAW and simple attenuation baselines establish
that the dose gain is not merely a consequence of choosing Ridge as a weak
comparator. The same evidence does not support a universal advantage of
every PHYSICS feature or learner. The penetration improvement emerges
mainly after adding constitutive proxies, and XGBoost’s narrow CV advantage
over HistGB should not be overinterpreted. Nested validation and repeated
splits would estimate development variability more fully, but the existing
single-split test must not be relabeled as such an experiment.
4.4. Numerical labels and physical validity
The MCNP diagnostic audit replaces a general defense of imperfect statis-
tics with case-level evidence. Forty-three runs fail at least one fluctuation
diagnostic, yet removing affected training labels barely changes the fixed-
model sensitivity result. This supports transparency and targeted inspection
rather than an automatic claim that all failed diagnostics invalidate learning.
It also does not excuse unresolved transport definitions. The AP conversion,
source activity, material composition conventions, and detector geometry
remain assumptions of the numerical reference.
On the impact side, normal termination and a satisfactory energy ratio are
computational acceptance checks rather than a complete assessment of model
adequacy. Finite observation time, a one-element projectile, constitutive
assumptions, and recovery provenance limit the physical interpretation. The
two longer runs add useful time-history evidence without establishing mesh
or temporal convergence for the campaign. Consequently, optimized config-
urations should be treated as candidates for further numerical assessment,
with conclusions confined to the modeled conditions.
26
5. Conclusions
The study supports a computational design workflow for a constrained
gamma–fragment shielding problem, with five substantive conclusions.
1. Physics-guided inputs make the modeled dose response easier to learn
even against the best tuned RAW model and simple attenuation base-
lines. The smaller ballistic gain and its high-error tail show that repre-
sentation quality must be judged separately for each physical response.
2. Optimizer-selected designs can expose errors that are not apparent in
a random test. The positive dose bias and reduced coverage in the
thirty re-evaluations make direct solver checks essential before relying
on predicted improvements.
3. The calibrated penetration allowance is a transparent constant back-off,
but did not affect the inspected feasible set at the operational 60 mm
limit. Its benefit cannot be inferred from successful implementation
alone. Re-optimization at tighter thresholds changes selected designs
and rejects some point-feasible front members, but does not establish a
Holm-adjusted HV difference. Changes in design identity alone are not
evidence of a safety gain.
4. Equal-budget comparisons show that evolutionary search improves HV
relative to the bounded random archive, while NSGA-III improves
it further than NSGA-II under the tested settings. The framework
therefore supports substitution of its optimizer, rather than depending
on a unique algorithmic winner. This conclusion concerns surrogate-
space search, not solver confirmation of the new front.
5. Numerical re-evaluation supports improvement beyond the initial design
set, while the observed mass range restricts the application to substantial
supported barriers. The result is a set of computationally promising
alternatives, not evidence of a wearable shield or a globally optimal
design.
These conclusions locate the contribution in the integration and audit of
multifunctional design decisions. Extending the framework to other radiation
fields, fragmentconditions, materiallibraries, ordeploymentenvelopesrequires
scenario-specific simulation, model development, and validation rather than
direct transfer of the present uncertainty bounds.
27
Limitations
The study has one development split, nonnested model-selection CV, and
an offline constant-width conformal construction. The additional optimizer
benchmark covers three specified search methods at one population and
evaluation budget rather than an exhaustive or independently tuned algorithm
comparison. Conditional bootstrap intervals and post-review sensitivity fits
do not remove those limitations. The thirty candidates are purposively
selected, and their interval coverage is descriptive rather than a population-
level guarantee. Four missing ballistic labels, concentrated in thin outer-Al
designs, may introduce design-dependent selection bias.
The physical evidence is simulation-based. It does not include a mesh-
convergence study or a broad terminal-time convergence study for the actual
material stack and cubical FSP. The retained labels combine documented
campaign and recovery sources. Gamma-only transport with AP conversion,
a fixed source activity, and a nonanatomical detector does not constitute full
responder dosimetry. Blast, multiple fragments, manufacturing variability,
neutron exposure, and operational deployment remain outside the scope. The
costcoefficientsarefixedcomparativeinputsratherthanverifiedcontemporary
market prices.
